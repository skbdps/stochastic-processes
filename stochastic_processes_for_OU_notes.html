0. How to Read These Notes

The project these notes serve

Everything here exists for one goal: estimating the parameters (θ, μ, σ) of an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

dXt = θ(μ − Xt) dt + σ dBt

from data observed at irregular time gaps Δi, by exact maximum likelihood — and quantifying the bias of the "naive" estimator that pretends all gaps equal their mean. These notes cover the foundations built so far: everything up to and including Brownian motion, ending exactly at the doorway where SDE notation gets defined.

1. MLE Mechanics (Revision)

Where this sits: the entire project is one giant MLE computation, so the mechanics were re-sharpened first. Four facts, each with the exact phrasing worth keeping.

1.1 max vs argmax: height vs location

  • Every point of a graph has an input (θ, x-axis) and an output (function value, y-axis).
  • max f = the height of the highest point (a y-value). argmax f = the θ-location of the highest point (an x-value). "arg" = argument = input.
  • The MLE is a parameter value, so MLE = argmax, never max. For 7 heads in 10 flips: max of the likelihood ≈ 0.267 (unreported height); argmax = 0.7 (the estimate).

1.2 The log is legal because it does not move the argmax

  • log is monotonically increasing: if P(D|θ) is largest at some θ, log P(D|θ) is largest at the same θ.
  • Precision worth keeping: the log changes the height of the peak, not its location. Say "log doesn't move the argmax," not "doesn't change the maximum."
  • Practical necessity, not just convenience: a product of hundreds of factors each < 1 underflows to zero in floating point; the log converts it into a survivable sum.

1.3 argmax f = argmin (−f): the minus attaches to the function, not the answer

argmaxθ f(θ) = argminθ [−f(θ)]

  • Negation flips the graph upside down: the peak becomes a valley at the same θ. Vertical-only transformation — nothing moves left or right.
  • Worked check (coin, 7 heads / 10 flips, L(θ) = 120 θ7(1−θ)³):
    θL(θ)−ln L(θ)
    0.00∞
    0.10.000008711.65
    0.30.00904.71
    0.50.11722.14
    0.70.26681.32
    0.90.05742.86
    1.00∞
    argmax of column 2 = argmin of column 3 = 0.7. argmin of column 2 = {0, 1}: the parameters that make the data impossible — the worst explainers, useless.
  • Why we minimize at all: optimizers are downhill machines (w −= lr·grad; scipy's minimize; L-BFGS-B). The minus sign must live somewhere — the ecosystem puts it in the objective ("negative log-likelihood"), keeping every optimizer a pure minimizer. Mathematically optional, practically universal.
  • Bonus geometry: after negation + log, the worst parameters (θ = 0, 1) become infinite walls (−ln 0 = +∞) — the loss landscape actively repels the optimizer from garbage.
The full chain, justified end to end:

θ̂MLE = argmaxθ P(D|θ) = argmaxθ log P(D|θ) = argminθ [−log P(D|θ)]

First equality: definition. Second: log is monotone. Third: negation flips peak to valley in place.

2. The Simple Random Walk

Where we are → what's new: all of Sessions 1–7 dealt with randomness delivered at once (a coin flipped, a bolt measured). The project's world — prices, rates — receives randomness over time, kick after kick. The random walk is the smallest object with that structure, and it is the seed of everything that follows.

2.1 The construction: two layers, keep them straight

  • Layer 1 — the steps. S1, S2, … i.i.d. (independent, identically distributed). Simplest instance: P(Si = +1) = P(Si = −1) = ½. General: any distribution with finite mean μS and variance σS².
  • Layer 2 — the positions. W0 = 0, Wk = Σi=1k Si.
  • The steps are independent; the positions are dependent. Both statements hold at once because they are about different layers. Proof of dependence by counterexample: P(W4 = 4) = 1/16, but P(W4 = 4 | W3 = 3) = ½ — conditioning changed the distribution, so W3, W4 are dependent. Mechanism: shared summands (W4 contains all of W3's steps). The only fresh randomness in W4 beyond W3 is the increment W4 − W3 = S4.

2.2 The walk's statistics — general form first, instances second

For i.i.d. steps with mean μS, variance σS²:

E[Wk] = k μS      Var(Wk) = k σS²

  • Mean: linearity of expectation (S3) — needs no assumptions, holds under any dependence.
  • Variance: variances add for independent summands (S3). The precondition is consumed by the steps (the summands), not the positions — the positions' dependence never enters.
  • Consequence for the ±1 walk (μS = 0, σS² = 1): E[Wk] = 0, Var(Wk) = k, σ(Wk) = √k.
The √-law: variance grows linearly in time, spread grows as √time. Four times the duration buys only twice the spread. Every "σ² × (time-like quantity)" in the project's crib sheet is this law in different clothes.

2.3 The shape: Gaussian, and why summing destroys the step's shape

  • Wk is a sum of k i.i.d. finite-variance variables ⇒ by the CLT (sum version — not the "sample means" version, whose variance shrinks instead of growing): Wk ≈ N(kμS, kσS²). Second slot = variance, always.
  • The mechanism (why bell-shaped regardless of the step's own shape): to land at m after k steps needs (k+m)/2 ups; every specific k-step sequence has identical probability (½)k; so P(Wk = m) = C(k, (k+m)/2)(½)k — the shape is entirely the binomial coefficients. Middle positions are reachable by combinatorially many orderings, extremes by few. The bell is arrangement counting; the step's shape enters only through the flat factor.
  • For finite k the distribution is discrete and only Gaussian-shaped; the statement is a limit. (This approximation upgrades to an exact identity for Brownian motion — §9.)

2.4 The three properties (MIT L5's Prop, in our notation)

PropertyStatementWhy
(i) No driftE[Wk] = 0 (for μS=0)Mirror-flip every path: same probability, opposite sign; paths cancel in pairs. Does NOT say the walk stays near 0 — spread grows as √k.
(ii) Independent incrementsincrements over non-overlapping time windows are mutually independentEach increment sums its own disjoint batch of steps; disjoint batches of independent steps are independent. Positions overlap; increments don't.
(iii) Stationary incrementsWt+h − Wt ∼ Wh (same distribution) for every tAny length-h window sums h i.i.d. steps; which h steps is irrelevant, only how many. The coin doesn't age.
Distinction to keep: (iii) says the increments' law is time-invariant. It does NOT say the process is stationary — Var(Wt) = t grows, so Wt itself has no time-invariant distribution. The OU process will be stationary as a process (a strictly stronger property); that contrast is exactly what mean reversion buys.

3. Fork: Gambler's Ruin and First-Step Analysis

Why this fork exists: it arose from MIT L5, and it delivered three tools the project reuses constantly — worth more than the formula itself.

3.1 The problem and the object

  • Fortune performs a fair ±1 walk; the game ends at −A (ruin) or +B (target).
  • Define f(m) = P(hit B before −A | currently at position m). m is a position, not a time. (In the walk chapter k indexed time; here the argument of f is a fortune level. One symbol, two jobs across contexts — flagged because it genuinely confused us once.)
  • Boundary values, free: f(B) = 1, f(−A) = 0.

3.2 First-step analysis (the derivation)

Condition on the first step out of m — law of total probability (S2) over the next move:

f(m) = ½ f(m+1) + ½ f(m−1)

The substantive step: after moving to m+1, you are a fresh gambler at m+1 — the probability from there is the same function f, evaluated at the new position. What licenses dropping the time argument: independent increments (the remaining steps don't care about the past) + stationary increments (round 2 onward is statistically a brand-new game). Without those, f would need a time argument.

Direction discipline (a real error we caught and fixed): this conditions on the first step out of m — not on "ways to arrive at m." Conditioning on the last step in governs a different object (occupancy/density evolution — Einstein's update rule). In the symmetric walk the two accidentally give the same equation; bias the coin (p ≠ ½) and they split: first-step gives f(m) = p f(m+1) + q f(m−1) (coefficients on the landing spots), arrival-reasoning attaches them to the opposite neighbors — and the p→1 sanity check kills the wrong one. File as: forward vs backward — the discrete shadow of a duality that resurfaces once at P5.

3.3 Solving: harmonic ⇒ linear

  • Rearrange: f(m) = average of its two neighbors  ⇔  f(m+1) − f(m) = f(m) − f(m−1) — equal rises.
  • A function climbing in equal rises is linear (arithmetic progression of values). Two boundary points pin it:

f(m) = (m + A) / (A + B)    ⇒    f(0) = A/(A+B),   P(ruin) = B/(A+B)

  • Note the crossing: your win probability is weighted by the opponent's distance-to-ruin. Sanity: at fortune 30 with boundaries 0 and 100, f = 30/100 = 0.3 — nearer ruin than target, so < ½. (An error we made and corrected: 70/100 is the ruin probability.)
  • Biased coin: equal rises die at exactly the division-by-(q/p) step; consecutive rises become a geometric progression with ratio q/p; AP only if p = ½.
  • "Value = average of neighbors" functions are called harmonic; discrete harmonic on an interval ⇒ linear is the 1D baby case of a deep family. We consume only the baby case.
What the fork banked for the project: (1) first-step analysis — condition on one move, hand the rest to a fresh copy of the same function: the discrete engine behind every transition-density argument in P5; (2) the license for one-argument functions of state — the Markov idea met in its natural habitat; (3) forward/backward direction discipline.

4. From Step-Counting to Physical Time: the Calibration

Where we are → what breaks: the walk is indexed by step count k — nothing in it refers to time. To model anything physical, each step must occupy a duration δ, so elapsed time t corresponds to k = t/δ steps, giving

Var(Wt) = (t/δ) σS²

  • The incoherence: two modelers of the same physics, one with tick δ, one with δ/2, get variances differing by a factor of 2 at the same physical time t. But δ was never physical — it's the modeler's discretization choice. A model whose predictions depend on the modeler's discretization is not a model of anything.
  • The forced fix: for Var(Wt) to be δ-free, the step variance must shrink in proportion:

σS² = c δ   (c constant)   ⇒    Var(Wt) = c t   for every δ

  • Finer ticks must carry proportionally smaller steps (step size ∝ √δ, step variance ∝ δ — scaling by a multiplies variance by a²).
  • Degenerate alternatives, for contrast: σS² fixed ⇒ Var = tσS²/δ → ∞ (density flattens, all mass escapes: no limiting variable); σS² shrinking too fast (e.g. ∝ δ²) ⇒ Var → 0 (collapse to the constant 0: useless as noise). The calibration is the unique scaling giving a finite, nonzero, resolution-free limit.
  • Normalization c = 1 defines the standard object.
This one condition — step variance proportional to tick duration — is the entire bridge from discrete walks to Brownian motion, and (§9.2) it is also a working error-detector for published notes.

5. Markov Chains (MIT L5 §3)

Where we are → what's new: in gambler's ruin we repeatedly used one trick — "the future depends on where you stand, not how you got there." Mathematics promotes a reused trick to a definition and studies everything that has it. That definition is the Markov property; the finite-state package around it is the Markov chain.

5.1 The definition and the objects

  • Markov property (discrete time, discrete states): a process X0, X1, … on state space S is a Markov chain if for all n and all states,

    P(Xn+1 = j | Xn, Xn−1, …, X0) = P(Xn+1 = j | Xn)

    Both sides are S2 conditional probabilities; the property is an equality of two conditionals: given the present, the fuller history adds nothing.
  • Precisely what kind of independence this is: Xn+1 ⊥ (X0,…,Xn−1) given Xn — conditional independence. It is NOT "Xn+1 ⊥ past" unconditionally: day 2 and day 0 are correlated (P(X2|X0) genuinely varies with X0 — that's the whole point of computing it); the dependence flows through the present, and conditioning on the present blocks it. Also: consecutive moves are dependent in general (move 1 selects which row move 2 draws from); they are independent only given the intermediate state. The walk was the misleading special case where steps were position-blind, hence unconditionally independent.
  • State space S = the catalog of allowed values. [m] = {1, …, m}: a finite catalog relabeled by integers — labels are names for matrix indexing, not quantities.
  • Transition probabilities pij = P(Xn+1 = j | Xn = i). Subscript order: from i, to j. No n anywhere ⇒ time-homogeneity baked in (same jump table every tick — the chain analog of stationary increments). Each exit row is a full PMF: Σj pij = 1.
  • The matrix A, lecture's column convention: entry (row j, column i) = pij; each column is one state's exit distribution; columns sum to 1. Most textbooks store the transpose (rows sum to 1) — check before copying any formula from another source.

Running example (the machine): S = {1 = working, 2 = broken}, p11 = 0.99, p12 = 0.01, p21 = 0.8, p22 = 0.2:

A = [ 0.99  0.8 ; 0.01  0.2 ]    (columns sum to 1 ✓)

The walk is a Markov chain too — but on S = ℤ, infinite, so no finite matrix exists. Matrix machinery is what finiteness buys.

5.2 Multi-step transitions: route sums = matrix powers

  • Define rij(n) = P(Xn = j | X0 = i) — n is a horizon length; by homogeneity only the gap matters, not the start date. Base case rij(1) = pij.
  • Two-step derivation: a fixed route i → k → j has probability pik pkj — and the license to multiply is not "the moves are independent" (they aren't, unconditionally); it is: product rule (an identity) chains the conditionals, then Markov collapses each to one step back, then homogeneity makes each factor a table entry. With the intermediate state unknown, enumerate the mutually exclusive launchpads (law of total probability):

    rij(2) = Σk∈S pik pkj

  • Machine numbers: r11(2) = 0.99² + 0.01×0.8 = 0.9801 + 0.008 = 0.9881.
  • The identification: "sum over an inner index of products" is exactly matrix multiplication. In the column convention: rij(2) = A²[row j][col i]; iterating one tick at a time,

    rij(n) = Σk rik(n−1) pkj  ⇒  R(n) = A·R(n−1)  ⇒  R(n) = An

    The law of total probability over launchpads is the inner-index sum of matrix multiplication — that identity of shapes is the entire reason chains are studied with matrices.

5.3 Distribution evolution: μn = Anμ0

  • μn = column vector of state probabilities at time n: entry i is P(Xn = i); sums to 1. A known start is a spike (1, 0, …); an uncertain start is any mix.
  • One tick of evolution: new mass at j = Σi pij P(Xn = i) — total probability again — which is exactly the matrix-vector product:

    μn+1 = A μn   ⇒   μn = An μ0

  • "Columns sum to 1" is precisely the property that makes A map probability vectors to probability vectors (total mass conserved each tick).
  • Worked instance (TV-provider market, S = {a, b, none}): today μ0 = (0.5, 0.4, 0.1); quarterly answer μ3 = A³μ0; the two computations answer "endpoint distribution," with intermediates summed out.

5.4 The stationary distribution π

  • Algebraic definition (fixed point): π is a probability vector with

    A π = π

    One more tick changes it by exactly zero — an identity, like an eigenvector equation, because it is one: π is the eigenvector of A with eigenvalue λ = 1, normalized so entries sum to 1 (eigenvectors are defined up to scale; the probability normalization picks the scale).
  • Dynamic characterization (limiting column): the columns of An converge, as n → ∞, to π — every column, the same π. Machine: columns (0.99, 0.01) and (0.8, 0.2) at n=1 become identical (0.9876543, 0.0123457) by n = 30; exactly π = (80/81, 1/81).
  • The theorem (L5 Thm 3.3; consequence of Perron–Frobenius): if all pij > 0, the stationary distribution exists, is unique, and every column of An converges to it. The two constructions — fixed point and limit — provably produce the same vector; that is why the two names coexist.
  • Reading of "all columns equal": column i is the distribution given start i; identical columns = the starting state has stopped mattering. The chain forgets its origin. Guard rail: without all-pij>0 (e.g. two disconnected islands of states), forgetting can fail.
  • Why powers converge to the λ=1 direction: the other eigenvalues have |λ| < 1 (machine: λ₂ = 0.19), and powering kills them — 0.19n → 0. Long-run behavior of a chain = an eigenvector computation, same species as PCA.
  • Finite-n columns are approximations converging to π, never equal to it; π itself is exact and unmoving. Journey vs destination.

5.5 MLE on a chain: transition counts are everything

  • Unknown θ = (p11, p21) (the other two entries follow from row sums). Conditioning on x0, the log's likelihood is a product of one factor per observed transition; identical factors collect into powers of the counts:

    L(θ) = p11n11(1−p11)n12 · p21n21(1−p21)n22

  • Structure: two separate coin problems multiplied — a "state-1 coin" and a "state-2 coin," each parameter appearing in only its own block. Log → sum of disjoint blocks → maximize each alone (the coin's k/n calculus verbatim):

    p̂11 = n11/(n11+n12)    p̂21 = n21/(n21+n22)

    — observed transition frequencies out of each state. (Worked instance: counts 60/20/15/5 give 0.75 and 0.75.)
  • The interleaving order of the log never entered — the counts are sufficient statistics.
  • Forward flag: in the project this clean split does not happen — θ, μ, σ appear in every transition factor simultaneously, so no closed form exists; that is exactly why Week 2 uses a numerical optimizer.

6. The Likelihood Factorization: Chain Rule + Markov Collapse

Where we are → what breaks: MLE needs P(D | θ). For the coin, D was independent flips and the likelihood was a plain product of marginals. The project's observations are dependent by design — mean reversion IS dependence between consecutive values — so multiplying marginals writes the likelihood of the wrong model. The chain rule is how a joint probability of a dependent sequence is written at all; the Markov property is what makes it usable.

6.1 The chain rule of densities (S1) — an identity

  • The tool, upgraded to carry θ: from the definition of conditional density, p(a | c) p(b | a, c) = p(a,b | c) — the p(a,c) cancels. So the product rule holds verbatim inside any conditioned world: whatever sits right of the bar rides along, appended to every factor.
  • Apply repeatedly, treating the growing history as a block:

    p(x0, x1, …, xn | θ) = p(x0 | θ) · Πi=1n p(xi | xi−1, …, x0, θ)

    Expanded: p(x0|θ) × p(x1|x0,θ) × p(x2|x1,x0,θ) × … — each observation contributes one factor: itself given its entire past; the conditioning grows by one per factor.
  • Status: identity. Built by applying the two-variable rule n times; assumes nothing — no independence, no Markov, no Gaussian. Power and cost together: always true, but late factors condition on huge histories — unusable at scale until something collapses them.
  • Intuition (multiplying = intersection): each factor prices the next event inside the world where the story-so-far already happened; the product is the probability of the whole story. Any ordering works; time order is chosen because models supply conditionals in that direction.
  • θ's role: not data — the model-world label. Frozen θ (machine example) = nothing to estimate, factors can be written bare. Unknown θ (project) = the live variable; the same formula becomes a machine that maps each candidate θ to how probable the observed record is in that θ-world; MLE compares those numbers over all θ. Same log, different θ, different probability — the number moves because θ moves; the data never does.

The project is the path question: the dataset is a complete log; MLE prices the one route reality took. The matrix machinery answers a question the likelihood never asks.

6.3 The Markov property in density form (S2)

  • The obstacle: continuous values make every exact-value probability zero (S1 of the probability notes): P(X = x) = 0, so table-lookup factors are identically 0 and MLE dies. Carrier change, same as PMF→PDF: the transition density p(y | x, θ) — for each fixed current value x, a full density curve over next values y. One function replaces infinitely many table rows; likelihood factors are the curve evaluated at the observed point — finite, informative, θ-sensitive.
  • The property, transplanted:

    p(xi | xi−1, xi−2, …, x0, θ) = p(xi | xi−1, θ)

    — equality of functions, pointwise: the full-history curve and the one-step curve are the same curve. Older history doesn't shift, reshape, or rescale it. Correct causal reading: the past is not erased; its influence is entirely mediated by the present (it helped determine today's value); given today, no residual channel remains.
  • Worked verification (AR(1) temperature, θ = (μ, φ, σ⊂ε⊂²) = (25, 0.5, 4)): the recursion Xi = 25 + 0.5(Xi−1 − 25) + εi gives, for today = 28: Xi | 28 ∼ N(26.5, 4), density at observed 26.5 = 1/√(8π) ≈ 0.1995. Add "yesterday = 31" to the conditioning: the generating rule contains Xi−1 and fresh ε only — Xi−2 appears nowhere and ε is independent of it — same curve, same 0.1995. Computed, not asserted.
  • Status: a property, not an identity. Some processes have it, some don't. Counterexample (momentum): Xi = Xi−1 + 0.8(Xi−1 − Xi−2) + εi needs two lags — given (28, 31) the curve centers at 25.6, given only 28 it's a different object: not Markov (in one variable; order-2 memory). The AR(1) has the property by construction; the OU process has it as a theorem, provable at P4 (the solution formula visibly depends only on current value + fresh noise).

6.4 The master formula

Substitute the collapse into every factor of the chain rule:

p(x0, x1, …, xn | θ) = p(x0 | θ) · Πi=1n p(xi | xi−1, θ)

  • Structure identical to the machine's product; carrier upgraded from table entries to transition densities evaluated at observed points.
  • Every factor conditions on one predecessor — the collapse's work; the record's length never increases any factor's arity.
  • This is the formula the entire project stands on: D5 is it verbatim with OU Gaussian factors (mean μ + (xi−1−μ)e−θΔi, variance (σ²/2θ)(1−e−2θΔi)); the Week-2 code is its logarithm — a sum of n one-step terms; the lone first factor p(x0|θ) is where the initial-condition decision lives (condition on x0 and drop it, or model it by the stationary law — a genuine choice deferred to D5).

7. The Gaussian AR(1)

Where we are → what's new: the master formula needs each one-step factor to be a concrete density. The AR(1) is the simplest process whose transition density is Gaussian — and it is the discrete-time skeleton of the OU process: the merge (M1) will literally be "the OU observed at gaps Δi is an AR(1) with gap-dependent coefficients."

7.1 Vocabulary (paper-grade, replacing all scaffolding words)

  • Long-run mean μ (= stationary mean; in the SDE literature, mean-reversion level): E[Xi] = μ in the stationary regime, and limk→∞ E[Xi+k | Xi = x] = μ from any x. Referee-proof precision: μ is not the unconditional mean at all times — started at fixed x0 ≠ μ, E[X1] = μ + φ(x0−μ) ≠ μ. It is the level expectations converge to.
  • Mean reversion: E[Xi | Xi−1 = x] lies strictly between x and μ — the expected one-step move is directed toward μ, proportional to the deviation; realized moves can still go either way (noise on top). Contrast the walk: E[Wk+1|Wk=w] = w — no home, no pull.
  • Autoregressive coefficient φ: the fraction of the current deviation that survives one tick (0 < φ < 1 ⇒ mean reversion; smaller φ = faster reversion). Will also equal the lag-1 autocorrelation (§7.4 — earned, not assumed).
  • Innovation εi: the new, unforecastable randomness at step i, independent of the entire past.

Xi = μ + φ(Xi−1 − μ) + εi,    εi ~ N(0, σε²) i.i.d.,   εi ⊥ (X0,…,Xi−1)

  • "Auto-regressive": regresses on its own previous value; "order 1": one lag consulted. φ = 1 recovers the random walk (with Gaussian steps): AR(1) = the walk plus a leash.

7.2 The k-step solution (derived by iterating the recursion)

Start at fixed X0 = x0. Substituting the recursion into itself and tracking the accumulating powers (each innovation ages by one factor of φ per tick):

Xk = μ + φk(x0 − μ) + Σj=1k φk−j εj

  • Check at k = 2: φ¹ε1 + φ⁰ε2 ✓ — oldest innovation carries the highest power, freshest carries 1.
  • Moments (linearity for E; variances of independent scaled terms add, with the scaling squared):

    E[Xk] = μ + φk(x0 − μ)     Var(Xk) = σε² (1 − φ2k) / (1 − φ²)

    (geometric sum Σr=0k−1(φ²)r; sanity: k=1 gives σε², k=2 gives σε²(1+φ²) ✓)
  • Gaussianity is exact at every k — a constant plus a sum of independent scaled Gaussians is Gaussian (S5 closure; no CLT, no limit needed):

    Xk ~ N( μ + φk(x0−μ),   σε²(1−φ2k)/(1−φ²) )

7.3 The stationary distribution

Send k → ∞ with 0 < φ < 1: φk → 0 and φ2k → 0, so

Xk  ⟶  π = N( μ,  σε²/(1−φ²) )

  • Both credentials, same as the chain's π: limiting object (any start x0, same destination — the memory of x0 vanished with φk), and fixed point (feed Xi−1 ~ π through one tick: mean μ + φ(μ−μ) = μ ✓; variance φ²σε²/(1−φ²) + σε² = σε²/(1−φ²) ✓). Density where the chain had a vector.
  • Anatomy = a tug of war: numerator σε² = noise injected per tick (spreads); denominator 1−φ² = the leash (contracts). Strong pull (φ→0): variance → σε². Weak pull (φ→1): variance blows up — and at φ = 1 exactly you recover the walk, whose variance grew without bound. A stationary variance exists because mean reversion beats noise accumulation; the walk is the boundary case where it doesn't.
  • Forward substitution (M1 preview): φ = e−θΔ with the matching innovation variance turns this into the OU stationary law N(μ, σ²/2θ); the mean's decay φk(x0−μ) becomes e−θΔ(x0−μ).

7.4 The autocorrelation function: ρ(k) = φk

  • In the stationary regime, lag 1: Cov(Xi, Xi+1) = Cov(Xi, μ + φ(Xi−μ) + εi+1). Constants drop; bilinearity; Cov(Xi, εi+1) = 0 by the innovation's independence of the past (the model's defining assumption); Cov(Xi, Xi) = Var. So Cov = φ Var, and dividing by the (equal) standard deviations:

    ρ(1) = φ

    — the autoregressive coefficient IS the lag-1 autocorrelation, the promised identity, now earned.
  • Lag k: from the k-step solution started at Xi, covarying with Xi kills the entire innovation sum (each ε ⊥ Xi), leaving

    Cov(Xi, Xi+k) = φk Var(X)  ⇒  ρ(k) = φk

    Geometric decay of memory: each extra tick multiplies the correlation by φ (at φ = 0.5: neighbors 0.5, two apart 0.25, five apart ≈ 0.03).
  • Forward: φ = e−θΔ makes ρ(k) = e−θ·kΔ — correlation decays exponentially in elapsed time: exactly D3's autocorrelation and the quantity Cell 1's assert checks (e−0.1 ≈ 0.9048).

7.5 Conditional MLE of the AR(1) flagged: revisit before M2

You flagged this as not yet solid; it is recorded here in full for the revisit, and nothing before M1–M2 consumes it.

  • Step 1 — likelihood via the master formula. Two legal treatments of p(x0|θ): keep it (modeled by the stationary law — "full/unconditional MLE") or condition on x0 and drop it (conditional MLE — the project's choice; cost: discards one observation's information, negligible against n transitions):

    L(θ) = Πi=1n p(xi | xi−1, θ)

  • Step 2 — each factor is the S2 Gaussian: Xi | xi−1 ~ N(mi, σε²), with conditional mean mi = μ + φ(xi−1−μ) — the model's one-step-ahead prediction.
  • Step 3 — log-likelihood:

    ℓ(θ) = −(n/2) log(2πσε²) − (1/2σε²) Σi (xi − mi)²

    For fixed σε², maximizing ℓ = minimizing the sum of squared one-step prediction errors: Gaussian + MLE = least squares, exactly the S5 MSE derivation in time-series clothes.
  • Step 4 — closed form via the regression trick: mi = μ(1−φ) + φxi−1 = a + b xi−1 — simple linear regression of today on yesterday ("auto-regression," literally). Solutions:

    φ̂ = sample Cov(xi−1, xi) / sample Var(xi−1)     μ̂ = â/(1−φ̂)     σ̂ε² = mean squared one-step prediction error

    Each estimator is a moment made empirical: φ̂ measures ρ(1); μ̂ the long-run mean; σ̂ε² the innovation variance. Theory (§7.3–7.4) and estimation lock together.
  • Where it breaks for the project (the punchline): with gaps, φi = e−θΔi and every transition has its own variance vi(θ) inside every term, tangled with the same θ — no collecting into a + bx, no closed form:

    ℓ = −½ Σi [ log(2πvi) + (xi − μ − e−θΔi(xi−1−μ))² / vi ]

    — the exact reason Week 2 hands this to L-BFGS-B. The naive estimator (Δi → mean gap) is the temptation to restore the constant-coefficient world — and inherit the bias the paper quantifies.

8. Path Space (MIT L17 §1)

Where we are → what breaks: every discrete process so far was specified by its increments — "say what one tick does; chain the ticks." The silent prerequisite: a next moment exists. In continuous time it doesn't (between any two times lie uncountably many more), so there is no "one tick" whose description generates everything. The recipe dies; the framework must change.

8.1 The replacement strategy: axiomatize, then outsource existence

  • Since a continuous-time distribution can't be built tick by tick, it is pinned down by properties: state a short list of demands, then (separately) prove some distribution over paths satisfies them.
  • The notes are candid that the second half — the construction (Wiener measure) — "requires a non-trivial amount of work and is beyond the scope of this class." Our ledger carries the same line as the substrate declaration: BM exists with its stated properties — definition-layer, cited not derived.
  • Internalize the pattern: axioms now, existence outsourced — the standard architecture of the subject, not laziness.

8.2 The formal object: a function-valued random variable

  • Definition: a stochastic process is a map

    X : Ω → { real functions on [0, ∞) }

    Outcome in, entire function of time out. One draw of randomness produces one whole trajectory.
  • This is the random-variable architecture with the target upgraded (your own catch, confirmed): RV: Ω → ℝ (number out); random vector: Ω → ℝd; stochastic process: Ω → function space. Same skeleton, different targets; "random variable" is conventionally reserved for the ℝ-valued case, so the function-valued case gets its own name.
  • Two views of one process:
    • View 1 (time-indexed family; ours until L17): for each fixed t, a random variable Xt: Ω → ℝ. Fix a time, get a random number.
    • View 2 (path space; the lecture's): one random object whose value is a whole trajectory. Make one draw, get a random function. View 1's slices are recovered by evaluation: Xt(ω) = the drawn path ω, read at time t.
  • Convenience convention: take Ω = the path space itself — each outcome ω is a path — so P directly weights sets of trajectories:

    P(X ∈ A) = P(X−1(A)),    A = a set of paths

    Events are no longer statements about one number; they are sets of functions (e.g. {paths staying below 5 up to time 3}; {paths hitting 100 before 0} — gambler's ruin's event in its native habitat).

8.3 Worked instances (2-step universe) and the key distinctions

  • Fair ±1 walk, two steps: Ω = {(+,+), (+,−), (−,+), (−,−)}, |Ω| = 4, P(ω) = ¼ each. The path (+,−) IS the function ω(0)=0, ω(1)=1, ω(2)=0.
  • Endpoint event {ω: ω(2)=0} = {(+,−), (−,+)}, P = ½ — matches the View-1 binomial count.
  • Whole-path event {ω: ω(t) ≥ 0 ∀t} = {(+,+), (+,−)}, P = ½ — native View-2 territory: marginal distributions of the slices alone cannot answer it.
  • Three objects, never to be conflated (an error made and fixed): ω = a whole path (a function); ω(3) = one number (that path's value at time 3); W3 = the evaluation map ω ↦ ω(3) (a random variable — the rule, not the path, not the number).
  • 3-step universe results (exercise, graded): |Ω| = 8, P(ω) = ⅛; {ω(2)=0} has 4 members (balanced first pair × free third step) ⇒ P = ½ — endpoint events ignore free steps; never-negative event = {(+,+,+), (+,+,−), (+,−,+)} ⇒ P = 3/8 — whole-path events constrain every step. That numeric contrast (½ vs 3/8) is the entire View-1-vs-View-2 lesson.

9. Brownian Motion (MIT L17 §2)

Where we are → what this is: the axiomatize-then-outsource strategy, executed. Brownian motion is defined by three demands; every one of them is a fact you already derived for the calibrated walk, promoted to an axiom of the limit object. (Notes' symbol B(t); our earlier Wt.)

9.1 Theorem 2.1 — the definition

There exists a probability distribution over continuous functions B: ℝ → ℝ satisfying:

  • (i) B(0) = 0 — anchoring. (Without it, B(t) alone would have no determined distribution; with it, B(t) = B(t)−B(0) inherits demand (ii).)
  • (ii) for all 0 ≤ s < t:   B(t) − B(s) ~ N(0, t−s) — two claims fused: increments are Gaussian, AND their law depends only on the duration, not the location (the walk's stationary increments, with the distribution now fully specified). Variance = elapsed time: the calibrated Var = ct with c = 1 ("standard").
  • (iii) increments over non-overlapping intervals are mutually independent — disjoint windows draw disjoint randomness (the walk's disjoint-batches fact, surviving the limit).

Three demands + path continuity; existence = outsourced (Wiener measure). Everything else about BM is a consequence.

Immediate consequences: B(t) ~ N(0, t); E[B(t)] = 0; Var(B(t)) = t; E[B(t)²] = t (variance + zero mean; no computation — parameters read off the parameterization).

9.2 The construction from the walk — pre-scaled (your fix as the definition), and the erratum

  • The model, stated correctly from the start: resolution n; ticks δ = 1/n; i.i.d. steps with E = 0 and Var(ξi) = 1/n = δ — the calibration σS² = cδ (c = 1) built into the model, not applied afterward. Concrete instance: coin steps of size ±1/√n. The process: Zn(k/n) = Σi≤k ξi, straight lines between grid points (continuity).
  • Demands verified: (i) exact at every n (empty sum). (iii) exact at every n (disjoint batches). (ii)-variance exact at every n: an increment over [s,t] holds (t−s)n steps of variance 1/n ⇒ Var = t−s — refinement invariance is a property of the model, nothing to rescue. (ii)-Gaussianity: the one thing only the limit supplies — sums of many i.i.d. steps sharpen to exactly Gaussian by the CLT (sum version). Formal reduction to the classical CLT: write ξi = ηi/√n with η i.i.d. variance-1; then Zn(1) = (1/√n)Σηi → N(0,1) verbatim.
  • The walk's approximate Gaussianity upgrades to an identity: at fixed t, the number of summands → ∞, the CLT's limit is reached: B(t) ~ N(0, t) exactly, for every t > 0.
Erratum (caught, proved, mailable): the L17 notes print the construction as Z(k/n) = Yk with variance-1 steps and claim Z(1) → N(0,1) "by CLT." As printed this is false — Z(1) = Yn has Var = n, and rigorously (mass-escape argument: P(|Yn| ≤ M) → 0 for every fixed M via the correctly-scaled CLT + continuity sets) Yn converges in distribution to no probability law. The intended definition is Z(k/n) = Yk/√n — whose translation is exactly the calibration: effective step variance 1/n = δ. Care point: "variance → ∞" alone is a red flag, not a proof (weak convergence doesn't control moments; counterexample Xn = G + n²·Bern(1/n)); the escaping-mass argument is the court-admissible version. Second erratum in this course (first: L8's stationary variance printed σ²/(1−φ), correct σ²/(1−φ²)). Submission channel: the MIT OCW contact form (ocw.mit.edu/contact), which accepts attachments.

9.3 The covariance function: Cov(B(s), B(t)) = min(s, t)  P1b

Derivation (the split — the one structural move): for 0 < s < t, write B(t) = B(s) + (B(t)−B(s)). Then by bilinearity:

Cov(B(s), B(t)) = Cov(B(s), B(s)) + Cov(B(s), B(t)−B(s)) = Var(B(s)) + 0 = s

First term: demand (ii)+(i) give Var(B(s)) = s. Cross term dies: B(s) is the increment over [0,s], B(t)−B(s) over [s,t] — non-overlapping ⇒ independent (demand (iii)) ⇒ covariance 0. Symmetry of Cov gives the order-free form:

Cov(B(s), B(t)) = min(s, t)

  • Meaning: the covariance equals the length of shared history — the two variables co-move exactly through the interval both lived through; everything after is fresh noise contributing nothing.
  • Importance: (1) mean function 0 + covariance function min(s,t) is the complete second-moment description — and for a Gaussian process, mean + covariance determine the entire law: this small formula, with Gaussianity, IS Brownian motion. (2) The technique — split → bilinearity → kill by independence — is verbatim the OU autocovariance derivation at P5 (ρ(Δ) = e−θΔ), the quantity Cell 1 asserts.
  • Companion computations: P(B(2) > 0 and B(5)−B(2) > 0) = ½×½ = ¼ (independent increments turn joint statements into products — the mechanism that makes D4's innovations independent across gaps and the likelihood factorize). E[B(t)²] = t — which is the Δ(s) ≡ 1 sanity check of the Itô isometry waiting in L18 (∫₀t 1 dB = B(t); isometry predicts Var = ∫₀t1²ds = t ✓).

9.4 The facts list, in our terms

  • Typical size = the standard deviation: B(t) ~ N(0,t) ⇒ |B(t)| ≤ √t with prob ≈ 68%, ≤ 2√t with ≈ 95%. The path lives in a parabolic sleeve widening as √t — the notes' "close to the curve x = y²" is this sleeve, axes (time x, value y): y = ±√x ⇔ x = y². (Making "the whole path forever" precise is the law of the iterated logarithm — out of scope; the working content is: typical size at time t = √t, the walk's √-law worn as the path's silhouette.)
  • Crosses zero infinitely often — mean 0 forever, noise never stops. Statement only; nothing downstream uses it.
  • Nowhere differentiable — next, because it is on the critical path.

9.5 Nowhere differentiability (Prop 2.4) — the fact that forces P2

  • Differentiability at t asks whether the difference quotient settles: Qh = (B(t+h) − B(t))/h.
  • Compute its distribution: numerator ~ N(0, h) by demand (ii) — location t drops out, duration-only; scaling by 1/h multiplies variance by 1/h² (S3):

    Qh ~ N(0, 1/h)  →  Var(Qh) = 1/h → ∞ as h → 0

    The quotient doesn't settle — it explodes. No slope, at any point, with probability 1 (Dvoretsky–Erdős–Kakutani for the "no point whatsoever" strengthening; the notes' own proof routes through the running-max machinery we scope out — the statement + mechanism is all downstream work needs).
  • The mechanism in one line: an increment of typical size √h divided by h has size 1/√h. A smooth function's increment has size (slope)·h, and dividing by h returns the slope. Increments of size h survive division by h; increments of size √h do not. The same √-law that made the variance resolution-free makes the derivative resolution-divergent — one law, two faces.
  • Real-life remark of the notes, which is the project's premise: any process is only ever observed at finitely many sample points — the continuous object accessed through discrete snapshots. That is literally the setting of the paper: a continuous OU path, observed at irregular times.

10. The SDE Notation (P2): the Differential Line, Defined as an Integral Equation

Where we are → what breaks: the project's central equation is written dXt = θ(μ − Xt)dt + σdBt. In ordinary calculus, "d" is shorthand for derivatives — but §9.5 proved dB/dt exists at no time, with probability 1. Read as calculus, the line asserts something about an object that isn't there: as it stands it is meaningless, and no manipulation of it is legal until it is given a meaning. P2 supplies the meaning.

10.1 The anchor: what differential notation already means in calculus

  • Take a plain ODE: x′(t) = g(t), x(0) = x0. Its differential form dx = g(t) dt reads: over a tiny interval of length dt, the change in x is rate × duration.
  • Recovering the function from the local statement = adding up the changes: chop [0, t] into pieces, sum the piece-changes, shrink — the sum becomes an integral:

    x(t) − x(0) = ∫₀t g(s) ds   (Fundamental Theorem of Calculus)

  • The differential line and the integral line are two notations for one statement; "d" was always shorthand for "will be summed." Instance: g(s) = 2s, x0 = 0 ⇒ x(t) = t²; the line dx = 2t dt never meant anything beyond this.

10.2 The audit: which reading survives for a noisy equation?

  • The OU model's verbal content over a small window [t, t+h]: change = pull toward μ + noise kick:

    Xt+h − Xt ≈ θ(μ − Xt)·h  +  σ (B(t+h) − B(t))

    Both right-hand objects exist: a number times h, and a Brownian increment ~ N(0, h). Nothing illegal yet.
  • Derivative reading (divide by h, let h → 0): drift term → θ(μ−Xt), fine; noise term = σQh ~ N(0, σ²/h) — explodes (§9.5). Dead exactly where it was proven to die.
  • Accumulation reading (sum over a partition 0 = t0 < t1 < … < tn = t, exactly as 10.1 did):

    Xt − X0 = Σk θ(μ−Xtk)(tk+1−tk)  +  σ Σk (B(tk+1) − B(tk))

    • First sum: a Riemann sum of the continuous path s ↦ θ(μ−Xs) ⇒ converges to an ordinary integral ∫₀t θ(μ−Xs)ds. Ordinary calculus, applied path by path. Nothing new.
    • Second sum telescopes — write three pieces: (B(t1)−B(t0)) + (B(t2)−B(t1)) + (B(t3)−B(t2)) = B(t3) − B(t0): every intermediate value cancels, only endpoints survive. For any partition of [0, t]:

      Σk (B(tk+1) − B(tk)) = B(t) − B(0) = B(t)   exactly, for every partition — no limit needed

  • Verdict: the derivative door is closed; the accumulation door is fully open, and every object behind it already exists.

10.3 The definition (P2 proper)

Since only the accumulation reading survives, the differential notation is defined to mean it. For coefficient functions a(x, t) and b(x, t):

dXt = a(Xt, t) dt + b(Xt, t) dBt   ⇔def   Xt = X0 + ∫₀t a(Xs, s) ds + ∫₀t b(Xs, s) dBs  for all t

  • A process Xt is a solution of the SDE iff it makes the right-hand (integral) equation true at every t.
  • The differential line has no independent meaning — it is compact notation for the integral line, exactly as dx = g dt was for the FTC. Every future manipulation of "d"-symbols must be justifiable on the integral side. That discipline is the entire content of P2.
Wrong frame to avoid (a correction we made): this is not an "anti-derivative" move. Nothing is differentiated and nothing is undone — dB/dt never exists at any stage. The definition is built from sums of increments (things that exist), not undone slopes (things that don't).

10.4 Worked examples — SDEs solvable by pure reading

  • Example 1, pure noise: dXt = σ dBt, X0 = 5. Integral form: Xt = 5 + σ∫₀tdBs = 5 + σB(t) (the telescoped sum). Distribution by S5 rules (constant shifts mean; scaling by σ multiplies variance by σ²; B(t) ~ N(0, t)):

    Xt ~ N(5, σ²t)

    A Brownian motion re-anchored at 5 and re-scaled — fully solved by reading.
  • Example 2, constant drift + noise (L17's Ex 2.6, "Brownian motion with drift"): dXt = a dt + σ dBt, X0 = x0, a constant. Integral form: Xt = x0 + ∫₀ta ds + σB(t) = x0 + at + σB(t):

    Xt ~ N(x0 + at, σ²t)

    Mean drifts linearly, spread grows as √t — the walk with μS ≠ 0, continuous edition.

10.5 The OU equation under the definition — meaningful today, not yet solvable

  • With a(Xs) = θ(μ−Xs) and b = σ constant:

    Xt = X0 + ∫₀t θ(μ − Xs) ds + σB(t)

    Every term is defined — that was P2's whole job. Read in words: position = start + accumulated pull + accumulated noise.
  • But the equation is self-referential: X appears on both sides — the unknown sits inside its own drift integral. Examples 1–2 escaped this because their right sides contained only known objects. Dissolving the self-reference is what "solving the SDE" means — P4's integrating-factor job.
  • Cost preview (why P3 exists): the solution will contain ∫₀t f(s) dBs with a non-constant deterministic weight f(s) = σe−θ(t−s) (old kicks damped, recent kicks fresh). Non-constant weights ⇒ the sum Σ f(tk)(B(tk+1)−B(tk)) no longer telescopes — each increment carries a different coefficient, nothing cancels — a genuinely new object. Defining it (as a limit of weighted increment sums: the walk's summing game with weighted steps) and deriving its law N(0, ∫f²ds) via the Itô isometry is precisely P3.

10.6 Status of the equation itself — where it "comes from"

  • The SDE is a postulate: the definition of the OU model (Uhlenbeck–Ornstein 1930 for particle velocities; Vasicek 1977 for rates) — exactly as the AR(1) recursion in §7 was postulated, never derived, and then its consequences were derived from it. The ladder derives the SDE's consequences (P4–P5); the equation itself is the modeler's axiom.
  • Kinship with AR(1) — same sentence, different clock:
    AR(1) (§7)OU SDE
    Timediscrete tickscontinuous
    The pullφ(Xi−1−μ) per tickθ(μ−Xt)dt per instant
    The noiseinnovation εiσ dBt
    Statuspostulated modelpostulated model
    Sign conventions flip appearances: φ multiplies the deviation that survives; θ multiplies the pull back. For small Δ, φ ≈ 1 − θΔ; exactly, φ = e−θΔ. AR(1) is not where the SDE comes from; AR(1) is what the SDE becomes at discrete observation times — and proving that is M1, the project's spine.

Solution. (a) A = [0.7 0.4; 0.3 0.6], columns sum to 1 ✓. (b) 0.7² + 0.3·0.4 = 0.61. (c) A²[1][1] = 0.61 ✓ (full A² = [0.61 0.52; 0.39 0.48]). (d) Aπ = π with π1+π2 = 1: 0.7π1 + 0.4π2 = π1 ⇒ 0.4π2 = 0.3π1 ⇒ π = (4/7, 3/7). (e) μ1 = Aμ0 = (0.55, 0.45).
Q5. Construct a process where the next value depends on the last two values; state what the chain-rule factor collapses to instead.
Solution. Momentum/trend model (e.g. Xi = Xi−1 + 0.8(Xi−1−Xi−2) + εi). The factor collapses only to p(xi | xi−1, xi−2) — order-2 memory; Markov in the pair (Xi, Xi−1).
Q6. (a) Full chain rule for p(x0,…,xn|θ). (b) Markov-collapsed version.
Solution. (a) p(x0|θ) Πi=1n p(xi | xi−1,…,x0, θ) — identity. (b) p(x0|θ) Πi=1n p(xi | xi−1, θ) — the master formula (§6.4).
Q7 (stretch). Weather table (§6.3 numbers): day 0 sunny, day 2 rainy, day 1 unobserved. Compute P(Rain1 | Sun0, Rain2).
Solution. Bayes inside the chain: numerator = P(Rain1|Sun0)P(Rain2|Sun0,Rain1) = 0.2·0.6 = 0.12; denominator = P(Rain2|Sun0) = 0.8·0.3 + 0.2·0.6 = 0.36 (computed in-session). Answer: 0.12/0.36 = 1/3.
Q8. Log of the Q6(b) factorization; what the log does structurally and why the argmax survives.
Solution. log p = log p(x0|θ) + Σi log p(xi|xi−1,θ) — product → sum of n cheap terms (numerically survivable); log is monotone increasing, so the maximizing θ is unchanged (§1.2).
