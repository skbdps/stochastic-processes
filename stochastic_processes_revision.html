00 · Orientation

One question holds the notes together

Suppose a temperature-like quantity tends to return toward a normal level, but unpredictable disturbances keep pushing it around. You observe 29, then 27, then 25.5. Is that evidence of a strong return toward 25? Before answering, you need one more piece of information: how much time passed between those readings? A fall of two units in a minute and the same fall in a day tell different stories about the speed of adjustment.

The eventual model is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, abbreviated OU:

dXt = θ(μ − Xt) dt + σ dBt.

For now, read it as a promise: change will contain a systematic pull and a random contribution. We will earn the meaning of the symbols rather than manipulate an unexplained differential equation.

Independent kicks → dependent positions → state-based prediction → path likelihood → mean reversion → continuous-time noise → an integral equation → the exact OU transition.

Sections 01–10 are the core revision route. They cover the scope of the original HTML through SDE notation. Sections 11–13 are an optional continuation into the mathematics used by the project. They are included to connect the foundations to the destination, not to imply that you have already mastered the later material. The separate repository document OU derivations remains the compact technical reference.

For variables with the required finite moments, E[aU + bV] = aE[U] + bE[V]. Independence is not needed for this identity. Variance is different: Var(U + V) = Var(U) + Var(V) + 2Cov(U,V). Independence makes the covariance term zero; scaling a variable by a multiplies its variance by a².

Conditional expectation E[Y | X = x] is the average prediction for Y after the information X = x is supplied. Conditional variance describes the uncertainty left around that prediction. The laws of total expectation and total variance say

E[Y] = E[E[Y | X]],Var(Y) = E[Var(Y | X)] + Var(E[Y | X]).

The second identity separates fresh unpredictability from variation in the predictions themselves. We will use exactly that distinction to find a stationary variance.

01 · Begin with the estimation question

What a likelihood measures

A probability model answers, “With these parameters, what data might I see?” Estimation reverses which quantity we vary. The data have arrived and remain fixed; we compare candidate parameters by the probability or density they assign to those same data. This function of the parameters is the likelihood.

Take ten independent coin flips with seven heads. For a particular ordered sequence, L(p) = p⁷(1 − p)³. For the event “seven heads, order not recorded,” multiply by the number of such sequences, 120. These are different observations and have different likelihood heights, but the factor 120 is constant in p, so both produce the same estimate.

ℓ(p) = 7 log p + 3 log(1 − p) + constant,ℓ′(p) = 7/p − 3/(1 − p) = 0 ⇒ p̂ = 7/10.

max is the height of the best likelihood; argmax is the parameter where that height occurs. We want the latter. Because log is increasing, it preserves the maximizing parameter. Negation changes maximization into minimization without moving the answer:

η̂ = argmaxη L(η)= argmaxη log L(η) = argminη [−log L(η)].

This is also numerically useful. Products can become too small or too large to represent; logs turn them into sums. The claim is not that likelihood factors are always less than one: density values can exceed one.

For continuous data, use density—not point probability

A continuously distributed observation has P(X = x) = 0. That does not make estimation impossible. If X has density fη, a small interval of width h near x has probability approximately fη(x)h. Comparing candidate models for that same narrow interval amounts to comparing fη(x), because h is common to the candidates.

f(y; m, v) = [1/√(2πv)] exp{−(y − m)²/(2v)},   v > 0.

The Gaussian density rewards a prediction close to y, but it also accounts for the claimed uncertainty. A huge variance reduces the squared-error penalty while spreading probability mass thinly; the normalization prevents “everything is possible” from being a free solution. Consequently, when a variance depends on parameters, its log term must not be dropped.

A likelihood is not a probability distribution over parameters. In frequentist estimation η is a fixed unknown. Writing L(η; data) keeps that distinction visible. A Bayesian posterior requires a prior and a separate normalization.

1. Does taking a log change the maximum?

It generally changes the maximum value, but not the maximizing parameter. For the seven-head count, the maximum probability is about 0.2668 and its logarithm is about −1.3212. Both occur at p = 0.7. The negative log has its minimum at the same p.

So far the coin observations were independent. Our next task is to build a model in which successive values are dependent, and then write its likelihood without pretending otherwise.

02 · Build dependence from independence

Random kicks, dependent positions

Let S₁, S₂, … be independent, identically distributed steps, with mean mS and finite variance vS. Begin at W₀ = 0 and accumulate them:

Wk = S₁ + ⋯ + Sk,   Wk+1 = Wk + Sk+1.

Imagine keeping a running total of independent daily accounting adjustments. Today's adjustment can be independent of yesterday's adjustment even though today's balance and yesterday's balance are strongly related. The balances share nearly all the same adjustments. A random walk has exactly this structure.

For a fair ±1 walk, W₄ = 4 has probability 1/16. Once you know W₃ = 3, the chance that W₄ = 4 rises to 1/2. Conditioning changes the distribution, so the positions are dependent. The fresh increment W₄ − W₃ = S₄ is nevertheless independent of the earlier steps.

Derive the spread rather than memorize it

E[Wk] = kmS,   Var(Wk) = kvS.

The mean follows by linearity. The variance follows because the summands are independent. For j ≤ k, split Wk into Wj and the new steps after j. Those new steps have zero covariance with Wj, giving

Cov(Wj, Wk) = Var(Wj) = jvS.

Covariance measures the shared portion of the accumulated history. That argument will return almost unchanged for Brownian motion.

For the fair ±1 walk, the mean is zero, the variance is k, and the standard deviation is √k. Zero mean means that positive and negative outcomes balance across repeated experiments. It does not mean that an individual path stays near zero. The collection of possible positions spreads out.

Where the bell shape enters

If vS > 0, the central limit theorem gives the normalized statement

(Wk − kmS)/√(kvS) → N(0,1) in distribution.

Thus, for large k, Wk is approximately N(kmS, kvS). A ±1 walk still lives on a discrete lattice at finite k, so this is not an equality of distributions. Near the center of the fair walk there are many more possible arrangements of up and down steps than at the extremes; the binomial coefficients make the Gaussian-shaped envelope visible. That counting story illustrates the Bernoulli case, while the theorem covers much more general finite-variance steps.

Two further properties are worth separating. Independent increments means that changes over disjoint time windows use disjoint independent steps. Stationary increments means that the law of a change depends only on the number of steps in its window, not on where that window begins. Neither statement says that the position has a stationary distribution: its variance still grows with k.

2. A walk with unequal step sizes

Let P(S = 2) = 1/4 and P(S = −1) = 3/4. Then E[S] = −1/4, E[S²] = 7/4, and Var(S) = 7/4 − 1/16 = 27/16. Therefore E[W₁₀₀] = −25 and Var(W₁₀₀) = 168.75. The CLT approximation is N(−25, 168.75); the second number is the variance, not its square root.

3. Which changes share randomness?

For independent unit-variance steps, W₅ − W₂ uses S₃,S₄,S₅ and W₈ − W₅ uses S₆,S₇,S₈. They are independent. But W₅ − W₂ and W₆ − W₄ share S₅, so their covariance is 1. Non-overlap matters; simply calling both objects “increments” does not make them independent.

Reference: MIT L5, random walks. The calculations above follow directly from the stated step assumptions.

03 · A useful detour

Think one step ahead

A gambler's fortune moves by ±1 and stops at 0 or N. Starting with m units, what is the probability of reaching N before 0? This is not a question about the position at a fixed time. It is a question about which boundary a whole path reaches first.

Define f(m) to be that success probability. The boundary values are immediate: f(0) = 0 and f(N) = 1. At an interior point, condition on the next step. For a fair walk:

f(m) = ½f(m + 1) + ½f(m − 1).

Why can we reuse f after taking a step? The unused steps are independent of the past and have the same distribution as before. After arriving at m + 1, the remaining task is exactly the original task with a different starting fortune.

Rearrange the equation to obtain f(m + 1) − f(m) = f(m) − f(m − 1). Success probabilities rise by the same amount for every extra unit of fortune. The boundary conditions force that amount to be 1/N:

f(m) = m/N.

At 30 with a target of 100, success has probability 0.3; ruin has probability 0.7. The equation is an example of a discrete harmonic function: its value equals an average of neighboring values.

A biased walk reveals why the direction matters

With an upward probability p and downward probability q = 1 − p, the correct recursion is f(m) = pf(m + 1) + qf(m − 1). These coefficients describe departures from the current state. If dm = f(m) − f(m − 1), the recursion becomes pdm+1 = qdm. Thus the rises are geometric, not constant. Summing them and using the boundaries gives, for 0 < p < 1 and p ≠ 1/2,

f(m) = [1 − (q/p)m] / [1 − (q/p)N].

Do not confuse this with updating an occupancy probability gk(m) = P(Wk = m). That forward update is gk+1(m) = pgk(m − 1) + qgk(m + 1): it counts arrivals into m. In the fair case the distinction is hidden by equal coefficients.

4. What changes if the target is −A or +B?

Shift fortune by A, so the boundaries become 0 and A + B. Starting at m becomes starting at m + A. The fair-walk probability of hitting +B first is (m + A)/(A + B). From m = 0 it is A/(A + B), not B/(A + B).

The reusable idea is not the gambler's formula. It is that the present state can summarize everything relevant for the remaining prediction. Next we make that statement precise.

Reference: MIT L5, gambler's ruin and first-step analysis.

04 · Compress the history into a state

What the present remembers

A Markov process is not a process with “no dependence.” It is a process for which the present state contains all the past information needed to predict the next state. In discrete time:

P(Xn+1 = j | X₀,…,Xn)= P(Xn+1 = j | Xn).

These conditionals agree on histories where the conditioning is meaningful. Older values may still be strongly associated with future values; their predictive influence is mediated through the current state. In probability language, the next state and the earlier past are conditionally independent given the present.

The choice of state matters. A position evolving with momentum may need both position and velocity. A recursion depending on Xn and Xn−1 may fail to be first-order Markov in Xn alone but become first-order Markov in the pair (Xn, Xn−1). A failed Markov assumption can mean that the state is missing information.

A transition matrix is a collection of conditional distributions

Use a two-state example with p₁₁ = 0.7, p₁₂ = 0.3, p₂₁ = 0.4, and p₂₂ = 0.6. Each pij is the probability of going from i to j. Following the original notes and MIT Lecture 5, put a state's outgoing probabilities in a column:

A = [ 0.7   0.4 ;   0.3   0.6 ],   Aji = pij.

The semicolon separates matrix rows. The columns sum to one. For a column vector rn of state probabilities, total probability gives rn+1 = Arn. A time-homogeneous chain uses the same transition matrix at every tick; a general Markov chain need not be homogeneous.

Starting in state 1, you can return to 1 after two ticks by either 1 → 1 → 1 or 1 → 2 → 1. Add the probabilities of these mutually exclusive routes:

P(X₂ = 1 | X₀ = 1) = 0.7² + 0.3 × 0.4 = 0.61.

This sum over intermediate states is exactly matrix multiplication. More generally, Aⁿ holds n-step transition probabilities. It is a compact way to sum over the middle states that were not observed.

A stationary distribution is a fixed point, not a promise of convergence

A probability column π is stationary when Aπ = π. If the starting state is drawn from π, another transition leaves its distribution unchanged. For our example, the balance equation 0.3π₁ = 0.4π₂, together with π₁ + π₂ = 1, gives π = (4/7, 3/7)ᵀ.

Whether other starting distributions approach π is a separate question. For a finite chain, irreducibility gives a unique stationary distribution; adding aperiodicity gives convergence from every starting distribution. Having every matrix entry positive is a convenient sufficient condition for both, but is not necessary.

Three ideas to keep separate: Markov means the state is sufficient for prediction; time-homogeneous means the transition rule does not change with the calendar; stationary means the joint distribution is unchanged by a time shift. A homogeneous chain need not be stationary when started at a chosen state.

5. Stationary—but no convergence?

Let A = [0 1; 1 0]. It alternates between the two states. The unique stationary distribution is (1/2,1/2)ᵀ, but starting in state 1 gives distributions (1,0)ᵀ, (0,1)ᵀ, (1,0)ᵀ,…, so there is no limit. The missing condition is aperiodicity. Conversely, if every column of A is already π, then Ar = π for every probability vector r. Convergence can occur exactly after one step; finite powers are not “never equal” to the stationary limit.

Reference: MIT L5 §3 for the column convention and its positive-entry convergence theorem. The counterexamples above can be checked directly by matrix multiplication.

05 · Predicting an endpoint is not pricing a record

The likelihood of a dependent record

Suppose you observe every state in a record x₀,x₁,…,xn. You are no longer asking which of many possible routes reaches an endpoint. The intermediate observations are known and must each contribute to the likelihood.

The probability chain rule, or its density version, always factors a joint law in time order:

pη(x₀,…,xn) = pη(x₀)× ∏i=1n pη(xi | x₀,…,xi−1).

So far there is no independence assumption and no Markov assumption. The factor for each observation conditions on its entire recorded past. Now impose the Markov property:

pη(x₀,…,xn)= pη(x₀) ∏i=1n pη(xi | xi−1).

The product comes from the chain rule; the short conditioning comes from Markov. Multiplying marginal densities pη(xi) instead would specify a different model, one that discards the dependence we built the process to represent.

For continuous states, a transition density pη(y | x) is a whole density curve over the next value y for each present value x. Evaluating that curve at the actual next observation supplies a likelihood factor. The event “the path equals these exact real numbers” still has probability zero; a joint density is what we evaluate.

What happens to the first observation?

A conditional likelihood treats x₀ as the supplied starting condition and uses the transition product. A full likelihood also specifies a distribution for X₀ and retains its density. For a stationary model that initial density generally depends on the parameters, so it cannot simply be called a constant and discarded.

Conditioning on x₀ is a legitimate modeling choice, not an approximation to the transition density. But the information lost relative to modeling X₀ is not automatically negligible in a short, highly persistent record. Keep the choice explicit. “Exact likelihood” can describe exact transition densities in either a conditional or a full formulation.

Transition counts give a simple Markov-chain MLE

With the initial state conditioned on, let nij count observed i → j transitions. The likelihood is ∏i,jpijnij, so its log is ∑i,jnij log pij. Maximize each outgoing distribution subject to ∑jpij = 1. The result is p̂ij = nij/∑jnij when the denominator is positive. If a state has no observed departures, its outgoing probabilities are not identified by this conditional likelihood. The counts summarize the observed transition factors; a modeled parameter-dependent initial distribution can change the optimization.

6. Endpoint probability versus a particular route

Using A = [0.7 0.4; 0.3 0.6] and starting in state 1, the probability of being in state 1 two ticks later is 0.61. The probability of the specific route (1,1,1), conditional on its start, is only 0.7² = 0.49. The extra 0.12 comes from the other route (1,2,1). Sum unobserved alternatives; multiply the conditional factors of an observed record.

7. Reconstruct an unobserved middle state

Use a homogeneous weather chain with S → S = 0.8, S → R = 0.2, R → S = 0.4 and R → R = 0.6. You observe S on day 0 and R on day 2. Then

P(R₁ | S₀,R₂) = (0.2 × 0.6)/(0.8 × 0.2 + 0.2 × 0.6)= 0.12/0.28 = 3/7.

The denominator sums the S → S → R and S → R → R routes. This question is completely specified here; it does not rely on a missing weather table or a previous conversation.

We now know the structure of the likelihood. To calculate it, we need a model that supplies its transition densities. The Gaussian AR(1) gives us the simplest useful example with a tendency to return toward a level.

06 · Add mean reversion

Give the process a home

In a random walk, the best prediction of the next position is the current position, plus any fixed step mean. There is no preferred level. To model return toward μ, retain only a fraction φ of the current deviation before adding new noise:

Xi = μ + φ(Xi−1 − μ) + εi,εi independently distributed as N(0, τ²),εi independent of X₀ and all earlier innovations.

This is an autoregression of order one: tomorrow is regressed on one lag of the process itself. Its Markov property follows directly from the recursion and the independence of the fresh noise.

With μ = 25, φ = 0.5 and τ² = 4, a reading of 29 gives a next-step distribution N(27,4). The current deviation is four units; the forecast retains half of it. A realized next reading can still exceed 29. Mean reversion describes a conditional expectation, not a rule forcing every observed move toward μ.

E[Xi − Xi−1 | Xi−1 = x]= −(1 − φ)(x − μ).

For 0 < φ < 1, the forecast lies between x and μ whenever x ≠ μ. Stable causal AR(1) models more generally allow |φ| < 1: a negative φ gives oscillation around μ. At φ = 0 the next reading forgets the current one completely; at φ = 1 the model is a Gaussian random walk and μ cancels out. A mean-reverting scalar OU process sampled at a positive fixed gap corresponds specifically to 0 < φ < 1.

Unroll the recursion and watch the noise age

Start at a fixed x₀. At the first step the noise contribution is ε₁. At the second it is φε₁ + ε₂. At the third it is φ²ε₁ + φε₂ + ε₃. In general:

Xk = μ + φk(x₀ − μ)+ ∑j=1k φk−j εj.

The initial deviation and every past shock are gradually damped. Independence makes the variances of the weighted shocks add, and each weight must be squared:

E[Xk | X₀ = x₀] = μ + φk(x₀ − μ),Var(Xk | X₀ = x₀) = τ² ∑r=0k−1 φ2r= τ²(1 − φ2k)/(1 − φ²),   |φ| < 1.

The conditional distribution is exactly Gaussian, not merely approximately Gaussian: it is an affine combination of independent Gaussians. No central limit theorem is involved.

Stationarity balances retained noise against new noise

For |φ| < 1, the memory of x₀ fades and the variance tends to q = τ²/(1 − φ²). You can derive the same quantity without taking a limit. Suppose the current distribution has mean μ and variance q. The next value has variance φ²q + τ². Requiring it to remain q yields q = φ²q + τ².

X₀ ∼ N(μ, q),   q = τ²/(1 − φ²)⇒ the AR(1) process is stationary.

The initialization must be independent of future innovations. A fixed start is not stationary, even though its distribution converges to the same invariant Gaussian. Strict stationarity means every finite collection of values has a distribution unchanged by a common time shift. Weak stationarity only requires a constant mean and a covariance depending on lag; for Gaussian processes, those conditions also imply strict stationarity.

Correlation is the surviving fraction of a deviation

In stationarity, write Xi+k as μ + φᵏ(Xi − μ) plus future innovations. Those future innovations have zero covariance with Xi. Therefore

Cov(Xi, Xi+k) = qφk,   Corr(Xi, Xi+k) = φk.

The same parameter controls both forecast decay and correlation decay. This makes it measurable from a sufficiently informative time record.

8. Separate one-step, two-step and stationary variance

For μ = 25, φ = 1/2, τ² = 4 and X₀ = 29, X₁ is N(27,4), while X₂ is N(26,5), because 4(1 + 1/4) = 5. The stationary variance is 4/(1 − 1/4) = 16/3, not 8. The stationary mean is 25; the means of the first two readings from this fixed start are not 25.

Reference: MIT L8, AR(1) model. Its displayed stationary-variance typo is corrected by the derivation q = φ²q + τ² above.

07 · Turn predictions into parameter estimates

Fit the Gaussian AR(1)

Condition on the observed x₀. There are n transitions, each with conditional mean μ + φ(xi−1 − μ) and variance τ². Substituting the Gaussian density into the Markov product gives

ℓ(μ,φ,τ²) = −(n/2)log(2πτ²)− [1/(2τ²)] ∑i=1n [xi − μ − φ(xi−1 − μ)]².

For a fixed positive τ², maximizing this likelihood is equivalent to minimizing the sum of squared one-step prediction errors. The reduction works because every transition has the same variance. The observations themselves do not need to be independent.

The regression form makes the algebra transparent

Let ui = xi−1 and yi = xi. Write the predicted value as a + bui, where b = φ and a = μ(1 − φ). Define S(a,b) = ∑(yi − a − bui)². Setting its derivative in a to zero gives a = ȳ − bū, where ū and ȳ are the means of the lagged and next-value arrays separately. Substitute this into the derivative in b:

b̂ = ∑(ui − ū)(yi − ȳ) / ∑(ui − ū)²,â = ȳ − b̂ū,   μ̂ = â/(1 − b̂),τ̂² = S(â,b̂)/n.

The variance MLE divides by n, not the regression degrees-of-freedom adjustment n − 2. These formulas give the unconstrained conditional Gaussian-regression solution when the lagged array has positive empirical variance and the residual sum of squares is positive. Mapping back to μ also requires b̂ ≠ 1.

Check the parameter space before accepting the answer. An unconstrained fitted b̂ can lie outside |φ| < 1. For an OU interpretation it must lie in (0,1). An inadmissible unconstrained optimum is not repaired by silently clipping it and calling the result the same MLE; solve the constrained problem and report boundary behavior. A fitted φ near one also makes μ = a/(1 − φ) unstable.

The population stationary correlation equals φ, but the sample regression slope is not generally the sample Pearson correlation: b̂ = ruysy/su. The two sample standard deviations need not coincide. Likewise, the algebraic regression equivalence does not automatically justify every independent-design regression confidence interval for a time series.

9. Calculate a conditional fit by hand

Use the record (0,2,1,3). The lagged array is (0,2,1), its mean is 1; the next-value array is (2,1,3), its mean is 2. The centered cross-product sum is −1 and the lagged squared-deviation sum is 2. Thus b̂ = −1/2 and â = 5/2. Predicted values are (2.5,1.5,2), residuals are (−0.5,−0.5,1), and S = 1.5. Hence τ̂² = 0.5 and μ̂ = (5/2)/(3/2) = 5/3. The slope is admissible for a stable oscillating AR(1), but not for the positive mean-reverting OU model.

At this point the statistical machinery is ready. The remaining question is how to describe a process between observation times, especially when there is no common tick Δ.

08 · Let the clock become finer

Refine time without changing the noise

A model indexed by step count does not yet distinguish one second from one day. Suppose each numerical tick lasts δ time units. If every tick retains unit-variance noise, then at time t there are approximately t/δ independent kicks, with variance approximately t/δ. Merely choosing a finer numerical clock would change the modeled uncertainty.

To obtain a finite nonzero diffusion-scale limit, make a kick's variance proportional to its duration. For centered, unit-variance independent ξi, use

one kick = aδ + σ√δ ξi.

At a grid time t = kδ, the accumulated mean is kaδ = at and the variance is kσ²δ = σ²t. Drift scales with duration; random spread scales with the square root of duration. This is a modeling regime, not a claim that every discrete-time stochastic model must ignore its physical tick size.

There are two levels of convergence

For a fair walk with unit-variance steps ξi, let Wk = ∑i=1kξi and define Zn(k/n) = Wk/√n. The ordinary CLT explains why the distribution at a fixed time approaches a Gaussian. A functional central limit theorem is the stronger result asserting convergence of the suitably interpolated whole random path to Brownian motion. The full theorem is used here as a theorem, not proved by a one-time CLT calculation.

Why the interpolated walk is not already Brownian motion

Join consecutive grid points by straight lines. At t = (k + r)/n with 0 ≤ r < 1,

Zn(t) = (Wk + rξk+1)/√n,Var(Zn(t)) = (k + r²)/n.

The target Brownian variance is t = (k + r)/n; the two agree on the grid but not everywhere between grid points. Two disjoint subintervals within one grid cell also share the same ξk+1, so their increments are not independent. These finite-resolution effects disappear in the limit; they cannot be asserted away at finite n.

One outcome is now an entire path

Think of a simulation that produces a whole trajectory. The random input is ω; the resulting curve is t ↦ Xt(ω). Fix a time t and Xt is a random variable across repeated simulations. Fix ω and the trajectory is an ordinary function of time. Fix both and Xt(ω) is one number.

This distinction matters because the endpoint distribution is not the whole process. “The value at time 3 is positive” is an endpoint event. “The path stayed positive throughout the first three units of time” is a different event involving the joint behavior across times. A family of one-time marginal distributions alone does not determine all such path questions.

10. A two-step path-space calculation

For a fair ±1 walk, the four step sequences (++),(+−),(−+),(−−) each have probability 1/4. Ending at zero selects (+−),(−+). Staying nonnegative at both recorded times selects (++),(+−). Both events have probability 1/2, but they are not the same event. Their intersection contains only (+−), with probability 1/4.

11. Check the interpolation error numerically

Take n = 4 and t = 1/8, halfway through the first cell. Then k = 0 and r = 1/2, so Var(Z₄(1/8)) = (1/4)/4 = 1/16, whereas a Brownian value at that time has variance 1/8. At t = 1/4, the grid-point variance is 1/4, as required. The normalization is necessary, but it does not make every finite interpolant Brownian.

Reference: MIT L17 §§1–2. Its walk display omits the factor 1/√n; the normalized construction and the grid/off-grid distinction are explicit here.

09 · The continuous-time noise model

Read Brownian motion correctly

Standard Brownian motion B is a process with B₀ = 0, continuous sample paths almost surely, independent increments over disjoint intervals, and Gaussian increments satisfying

Bt − Bs ∼ N(0, t − s),   0 ≤ s < t.

The law depends on elapsed time, not the starting date: its increments are stationary. Existence of a process with these properties is a mathematical theorem. We use that existence result rather than attempting to construct Wiener measure in revision notes.

Setting s = 0 gives Bt ∼ N(0,t). Brownian positions are not independent. For s ≤ t, write Bt = Bs + (Bt − Bs). The new increment is independent of Bs, so

Cov(Bs,Bt) = Var(Bs) = s,hence Cov(Bs,Bt) = min(s,t).

The covariance is the duration of shared history. The same decomposition gives Bt | Bs = b ∼ N(b,t − s). Brownian motion is therefore Markov: the latest value plus independent future increments determines its future law.

Every finite vector of Brownian values is jointly Gaussian because it is a linear transformation of independent Gaussian increments. For a Gaussian process, its mean and covariance determine its finite-dimensional distributions. Merely having Gaussian one-time marginals would not establish that joint Gaussian property.

Stationary increments do not imply a stationary process

Var(Bt) = t changes with t, so standard Brownian motion is not stationary. Also, a 95% interval such as approximately ±1.96√t concerns the value at one fixed time. It is not a 95% band containing an entire continuous path for all times.

Continuous does not mean differentiable

A differentiable function typically changes by an amount proportional to a small interval h. Brownian motion has a random change with standard deviation √h. Dividing by h yields

(Bt+h − Bt)/h ∼ N(0,1/h).

The family of difference quotients does not settle toward a finite ordinary slope. The stronger result is that Brownian paths are almost surely nowhere differentiable. That is a theorem, not a proof obtained merely by observing that the quotient's variance diverges. In particular, “explodes” should not be read as a claim of monotone pathwise divergence.

Optional intuition: why squared increments accumulate instead of disappearing

For a deterministic partition of [0,T] with interval lengths hj, let Q = ∑(ΔBj)². A centered Gaussian with variance h has squared value of mean h and variance 2h². Independence therefore gives E[Q] = T and Var(Q) = 2∑hj² ≤ 2T max hj. As the mesh tends to zero, Q tends to T in mean square. This is the basic quadratic-variation calculation behind Itô calculus. It explains why ordinary calculus rules can need extra terms for nonlinear functions of Brownian motion—even when a model's noise coefficient is constant. General Itô calculus is not needed for the linear OU derivation below.

12. Covariance, correlation and prediction are different numbers

Cov(B₂,B₅) = 2, whereas Corr(B₂,B₅) = 2/√(2 × 5) = √(2/5). Given B₂ = 3, the conditional law of B₅ is N(3,3), not N(0,5). The latter is its marginal law before observing B₂.

13. Calculate an overlapping-increment covariance

Let U = B₃ − B₁ and V = B₄ − B₂. Their intervals overlap on [2,3], so their covariance is 1. Expand it explicitly: min(3,4) − min(3,2) − min(1,4) + min(1,2) = 3 − 2 − 1 + 1 = 1. Disjoint Brownian increments are independent; overlapping ones generally are not.

Reference: MIT L17 §2 for Brownian motion and path regularity; L18 for the quadratic-variation context.

10 · The end of the core revision route

What an SDE actually says

If B has no ordinary derivative, the symbols dBt cannot mean B′(t)dt in ordinary calculus. The safe reading of a stochastic differential equation is an equation for accumulated changes. For an Itô SDE with suitable coefficients and an adapted solution:

dXt = a(Xt,t)dt + b(Xt,t)dBtmeans Xt = X₀ + ∫₀t a(Xs,s)ds+ ∫₀t b(Xs,s)dBs.

The first integral is an ordinary time integral along each realized path. The second is a stochastic integral. “Adapted” means that a quantity at time s uses information available by s, rather than looking ahead into future noise. Existence and uniqueness require conditions; the notation alone does not guarantee them for arbitrary coefficients.

With a constant noise coefficient σ, the stochastic part is especially simple. Every partition satisfies ∑(Btj+1 − Btj) = Bt − B₀ by telescoping. Thus ∫₀tσdBs = σBt. Nothing has been differentiated and then “undone.” We have summed increments that are well-defined.

First solve a model you can read directly

For dXt = a dt + σ dBt with fixed X₀ = x₀, the integral equation immediately gives Xt = x₀ + at + σBt. Therefore Xt ∼ N(x₀ + at, σ²t). A linear mean trend and a square-root-time standard deviation are simply the calibrated random walk's behavior in continuous time.

The OU equation is implicit—but now meaningful

Xt = X₀ + θ∫₀t(μ − Xs)ds + σBt.

The unknown X appears inside its own drift integral. That is the remaining obstacle: the notation has been defined, but the equation has not yet been solved. Solving it will express X using the initial condition and known noise without this self-reference.

Over a finite interval [t,t+h], its exact increment is

Xt+h − Xt = θ∫tt+h(μ − Xs)ds+ σ(Bt+h − Bt).

Replacing Xs by Xt inside the drift integral produces the Euler approximation θ(μ − Xt)h plus the same Brownian increment. It is not an exact equality for a finite h. When we sum such frozen-drift terms, they remain approximations until a justified limiting argument is made.

14. Translate, solve and interpret

For dXt = 3dt + 2dBt, X₀ = 1, the integral equation is Xt = 1 + ∫₀t3ds + 2∫₀tdBs = 1 + 3t + 2Bt. Hence Xt ∼ N(1 + 3t,4t). In the OU equation, the term θ∫₀t(μ − Xs)ds prevents this immediate direct reading because it contains the unknown process.

11 · Optional bridge · A weighted accumulation of noise

Weight the noise

The AR(1) solution weights old shocks by powers of φ. We should expect a continuous-time mean-reverting model to damp old noise too. The relevant object is ∫f(s)dBs: a sum of Brownian increments, each multiplied by a chosen deterministic weight.

Begin with a step function that equals cj on (uj,uj+1]. Define its integral, rather than guess its meaning:

I(f) = ∫ab f(s)dBs= ∑j cj(Buj+1 − Buj).

Each summand is a scaled centered Gaussian, and distinct increments are independent. Therefore the result is Gaussian with mean zero and variance ∑cj²(uj+1 − uj) = ∫f(s)²ds. Notice that the weights are squared before integration. Squaring ∫f(s)ds would be a different calculation.

The isometry makes the limiting definition reliable

For deterministic step functions f and g on a common partition, the same calculation gives E[(I(f) − I(g))²] = ∫(f − g)². Thus, if step functions fm approximate a deterministic function f in squared-integral norm, their random integrals get close in mean square.

Completeness of the space of square-integrable random variables supplies a limit. This defines I(f) for every deterministic f with ∫f² < ∞, independently of the approximating sequence. The identity carried to the limit is the Itô isometry:

E[(∫ab f(s)dBs)²] = ∫ab f(s)²ds.

The approximations are centered Gaussians whose variances converge. Their mean-square limit therefore has the Gaussian law

∫ab f(s)dBs ∼ N(0, ∫ab f(s)²ds).

Using a common partition also gives Cov(I(f),I(g)) = ∫fg. Integrals of deterministic functions supported on disjoint intervals are independent. An integral over a future interval is independent of the Brownian past and of an independent initial condition.

Deterministic weights are an important restriction. General adapted random integrands need a broader construction, and their integrals need not be Gaussian. The OU model below uses deterministic exponential weights, so this simpler theory is enough.

15. A piecewise weight and a smooth weight

First let f(s) = 2 on (0,1] and f(s) = −1 on (1,3]. Then I(f) = 2B₁ − (B₃ − B₁), with variance 4 × 1 + 1 × 2 = 6, so I(f) ∼ N(0,6). Next, ∫₀ts dBs has variance ∫₀ts²ds = t³/3, hence distribution N(0,t³/3). It is not obtained by treating dB as an ordinary ds.

References: MIT L18 and Särkkä & Solin §4.1. The deterministic step-function derivation is the route used here; general stochastic-integration theory is outside this companion.

12 · Optional bridge · The continuous analogue of damped shocks

Solve the OU model

Return to dXt = θ(μ − Xt)dt + σdBt, with θ > 0 and σ > 0. Subtract the target level: Yt = Xt − μ. The deterministic part is now the familiar decay equation dY = −θYdt. Its integrating factor is eθt.

The deterministic integrating-factor product rule, valid here because the factor is smooth and has finite variation, gives d(eθtYt) = σeθtdBt. Integrating and rearranging yields

Xt = μ + e−θt(X₀ − μ)+ σ∫₀t e−θ(t−s)dBs.

The formula says exactly what the AR(1) expansion said. The first term is the target. The second is the part of the initial deviation that survives. The integral contains all later shocks, with an exponential discount according to their age. A shock at s close to t is almost undamped; an old shock has less remaining influence.

Verify the solution in the integral equation

To avoid relying on formal differential manipulation, define Zt = ∫₀te−θ(t−u)dBu. Interchange the ordinary time integral and the Wiener integral for this bounded deterministic kernel on a finite time triangle. The relevant stochastic Fubini result applies here; this is an imported theorem with its conditions satisfied, not an unrestricted swap of integrals.

θ∫₀tZsds= ∫₀t[θ∫ute−θ(s−u)ds]dBu= ∫₀t(1 − e−θ(t−u))dBu = Bt − Zt.

Also θ∫₀te−θs(X₀ − μ)ds = (X₀ − μ)(1 − e−θt). Substituting the proposed X into X₀ − θ∫₀t(Xs − μ)ds + σBt reproduces the proposed X exactly. Two solutions driven by the same B with the same X₀ have difference Dt = −θ∫₀tDsds; the associated ordinary equation with D₀ = 0 has only the zero solution. This also verifies uniqueness in this linear case.

The transition density now follows from one variance integral

Restart the solution from an observed value x at time s. The future noise is independent of what happened before s. For any Δ > 0,

Xs+Δ | Xs = x ∼ N(mΔ(x), vΔ),mΔ(x) = μ + e−θΔ(x − μ),vΔ = σ²∫₀Δe−2θrdr= [σ²/(2θ)](1 − e−2θΔ).

The Gaussian law is exact because the weight in the stochastic integral is deterministic. It proves the Markov property and gives everything needed for exact simulation at observed times. There is no need to simulate thousands of hidden intermediate Euler steps just to generate this model's endpoint transition.

Check the formula at the extremes

For a tiny gap, e−θΔ ≈ 1 − θΔ. The mean becomes x + θ(μ − x)Δ to first order and the variance becomes σ²Δ to first order, recovering the Euler intuition. For a very long gap, the mean tends to μ and the variance tends to q = σ²/(2θ). Prediction eventually forgets the observed start.

If X₀ ∼ N(μ,q), independently of future Brownian increments, the whole OU process is stationary. Its covariance is q e−θ|t−s| and its correlation is e−θ|t−s|. From a fixed x₀ it is not stationary: Var(Xt) = q(1 − e−2θt). The approach to a stationary law must not be confused with stationary initialization.

The deviation's half-life is log(2)/θ. θ has units of inverse time, while σ has units of the state variable divided by √time. Thus σ²/(2θ) correctly has units of a variance. These unit checks can expose missing gaps or missing factors of two.

16. Recover a discrete-time AR(1)

At a fixed observation spacing Δ, define φ = e−θΔ and τ² = [σ²/(2θ)](1 − φ²). The exact observed OU process is Xi = μ + φ(Xi−1 − μ) + εi, with independent Gaussian innovations of variance τ². Its stationary variance is τ²/(1 − φ²) = σ²/(2θ). Its k-step correlation is φᵏ = e−θkΔ: the elapsed time is kΔ, not just Δ.

17. Do two transitions agree with one longer transition?

Let φ(h) = e−θh and v(h) = q(1 − φ(h)²). Two successive gaps h and k retain φ(k)φ(h) = φ(h + k) of the starting deviation. Their combined noise variance is φ(k)²v(h) + v(k) = q[φ(k)²(1 − φ(h)²) + 1 − φ(k)²] = q[1 − φ(h + k)²] = v(h + k). This is a strong check for the simulator and the transition formula.

References: MIT L21 §1.2; Särkkä & Solin, Examples 4.5 and 6.2; the repository's OU derivations.

13 · Optional bridge · The project's statistical question

Keep the observation gaps

Write the observed data as (t₀,x₀),…,(tn,xn), with Δi = ti − ti−1 > 0. The continuous-time OU rule is homogeneous, but its recorded one-step transition changes with each Δi. There is no single constant φ for an irregular record.

Observation-time assumption. The formulas here apply when the times are fixed in advance, or when the whole observation grid is generated independently of the entire OU path, including X₀. For a random grid we condition on its realized values. Sampling when the process hits a threshold is different: conditioning on that sampling event can change the observation law. Markov alone does not justify treating a hitting-time sample as an ordinary Gaussian transition at its realized gap.

Define φi = e−θΔi, mi = μ + φi(xi−1 − μ), and vi = [σ²/(2θ)](1 − φi²). The exact conditional log-likelihood is

ℓ(η | x₀, times)= −½∑i=1n[log(2πvi) + (xi − mi)²/vi].

Each transition contributes a forecast, an uncertainty and a realized prediction error. Long gaps mean more decay toward μ and more accumulated noise. The likelihood accounts for both; replacing every gap by its average changes both.

Three estimators differ in the transition they pretend generated the data

MethodConditional meanConditional varianceWhat changes?

These are two distinct approximation errors: ignoring spacing variation and approximating continuous-time dynamics. A small average gap is not enough to guarantee a good Euler approximation if some gaps are large relative to 1/θ. Equidistant sampling makes the exact and mean-gap objectives agree; it does not generally make Euler exact.

Irregular sampling still permits useful analytic profiling

A closed-form solution for the joint optimum in θ,μ,σ is not generally available. However, saying “everything must be optimized numerically” misses a useful reduction. Fix a candidate θ and set

bi = 1 − φi,   ci = (1 − φi²)/(2θ),di = xi − φixi−1.

Then the residual is di − biμ and vi = σ²ci. At this fixed θ, μ solves a weighted least-squares problem. Differentiate ∑(di − biμ)²/ci with respect to μ and set the derivative to zero:

μ̂(θ) = [∑ bidi/ci] / [∑ bi²/ci],S(θ) = ∑ [di − biμ̂(θ)]²/ci,σ̂²(θ) = S(θ)/n.

Substituting back leaves a one-dimensional objective:

NLLprofile(θ)= ½{n[log(2π) + 1 + log(S(θ)/n)] + ∑ log ci}.

This formula assumes S(θ) > 0. If the residuals vanish, a Gaussian likelihood can diverge as σ² approaches zero; no ordinary interior variance MLE exists there. The profiling formulas concern the conditional likelihood with free μ and positive σ. Extra constraints, priors or an initial stationary-density term can change this reduction. A one-dimensional search also needs appropriate bounds, boundary checks and convergence diagnostics; profiling is not a guarantee of an interior optimum.

Translate the equations into stable numerical operations

When θΔ is tiny, direct subtraction in 1 − exp(−2θΔ) loses significant digits. Use expm1(z), which computes exp(z) − 1 accurately near zero. The following excerpt illustrates one evaluation, not an optimizer or a replacement for the project's implementation.

# Inputs: positive theta, positive gaps, and x with len(x) = len(gaps) + 1.
# Validate finite values and dimensions before calling this calculation.
phi = np.exp(-theta * gaps)
b = -np.expm1(-theta * gaps)
c = -np.expm1(-2.0 * theta * gaps) / (2.0 * theta)
d = x[1:] - phi * x[:-1]

# Profile the level using gap-dependent inverse-variance weights.
mu_hat = np.sum(b * d / c) / np.sum(b * b / c)
residual = d - b * mu_hat
sigma2_hat = np.mean(residual * residual / c)
if not np.isfinite(sigma2_hat) or sigma2_hat <= 0:
    raise ValueError("Degenerate or non-finite profiled variance")

# Keep the variance normalization; it depends on theta and the gaps.
n = gaps.size
nll = 0.5 * (n * (np.log(2 * np.pi) + 1 + np.log(sigma2_hat))
             + np.sum(np.log(c)))

For a joint optimization, log-parameterization can enforce θ > 0 and σ > 0. Exact conditional simulation uses xi = mi + √vizi with independent zi ∼ N(0,1). At the true parameters and under the stated sampling model, standardized innovations are independent standard normals. Residuals computed after fitting parameters need not retain that exact finite-sample distribution.

Why ignoring irregularity can lower the inferred return speed

This is a large-sample illustration, not a finite-sample guarantee. Assume a stationary OU process, positive independent identically distributed gaps D independent of the entire path, a finite mean gap d, and the usual convergence of empirical regression moments as the observation horizon grows. Let q = σ²/(2θ). Consecutive sampled values have covariance qE[e−θD], so the constant-coefficient regression target is φ* = E[e−θD]. Mapping it to a constant-gap OU model gives θ* = −log(φ*)/d.

Because x ↦ e−θx is convex, Jensen's inequality gives E[e−θD] ≥ e−θd, and hence θ* ≤ θ. The inequality is strict for a nondegenerate gap distribution. For exponentially distributed gaps of mean d, direct integration gives E[e−θD] = 1/(1 + θd), so θ* = log(1 + θd)/d. With θ = 1 and d = 0.5, this is approximately 0.81093.

This explains one systematic distortion caused by an average clock. It does not prove that every finite-sample mean-gap estimate is below the truth, that exact MLE is unbiased, or that the asymptotic target determines interval coverage. The project's README identifies the mean-gap method as prior work rather than a new estimator; this calculation is a pedagogical derivation under the assumptions above.

18. Recover OU parameters from a valid fixed-gap AR fit

For 0 < φ < 1 and gap Δ > 0, θ = −log φ/Δ and σ² = 2θτ²/(1 − φ²). For example, φ = 1/2, τ² = 4 and Δ = 1 yield θ = log 2 and σ² = (32/3)log 2. A negative fitted φ cannot be inserted into this real-valued OU conversion.

19. Why can the log-variance term not be omitted?

If you keep only squared error divided by vi, increasing σ² makes every penalty smaller without limit. The log vi terms penalize that spreading and make a nondegenerate variance estimate possible. For fixed θ and μ, differentiating the full objective in σ² gives σ̂² = (1/n)∑residuali²/ci.

20. Does an exact model imply unbiased estimates?

No. “Exact” describes the transition law used by the simulator or likelihood. Bias, root mean squared error and interval coverage describe an estimator across repeated datasets. They still depend on sample size, total time span, spacing, parameter values and the inference procedure. A correct Gaussian transition does not by itself establish finite-sample accuracy or certify a confidence interval.
